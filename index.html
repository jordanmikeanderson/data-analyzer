About Data Analyzer

Data Analyzer measures how two columns in a spreadsheet move together. Load a CSV file, tidy it up, and pick two number columns. It draws the chart, fits a straight trend line, explains the result in plain English, and points out the rows that don't fit the pattern.

Everything happens in your browser. Your file is never uploaded, and closing the window clears it.

How to use it

  1. Open a file. Click Open CSV… or drag a file onto the window. No file handy? Click Try sample data for 1,000 Utah homes.
  2. Clean (optional). On the 1. Clean tab, keep the columns you need, drop rows with blanks, add filters, flag unusual values or shrink a big file to a random sample. Download cleaned CSV saves the result, and the cleaning log records every step.
  3. Analyze. On the 2. Analyze tab, pick Y (what you want to explain, like price) and X (what might drive it, like square footage). Not sure which X? The Strongest links to Y table ranks every number column. Click one to plot it.
  4. Read the results. Start with In plain English. Then check Rows to investigate for the points furthest from the line. You can download them, copy the results or save the chart as a picture.

Ways to use it

  • Accounting and audit: set an expectation (for example, expenses vs. revenue across months or locations) and look into the items that miss it by the most. This is the idea behind analytical procedures.
  • Real estate: see how price tracks size, age or lot size, and which properties look high or low for their size.
  • Business: sales vs. ad spend, hours vs. output, cost vs. volume, and which store or month breaks the pattern.
  • Data prep: cut a huge export down to a sample Excel can open, with a written log of what was removed and why.

What the numbers mean

Trend line
The straight line that fits the dots best. "Best" means the total of the squared misses is as small as possible (least squares).
Slope
How much Y changes, on average, when X goes up by 1.
Intercept
Where the line crosses X = 0. Often not meaningful on its own (a 0 sq ft house, for example).
R²
The share of the ups and downs in Y that the line accounts for, from 0% to 100%.
Correlation (r)
Strength and direction, from −1 to +1. Near 0 means no straight-line relationship.
p-value
If there were really no relationship, how often a slope this far from zero would show up just by chance. Under 0.05 is the usual cutoff for "unlikely to be chance".
Std. error of estimate
The typical distance between a dot and the line, in Y's units.
Confidence band
The shaded area where the true trend line most likely sits. Individual dots can fall well outside it.
Std. residual
How far a row sits from the line, measured in standard errors. Beyond ±2 is unusual (about 1 row in 20 by chance alone), and beyond ±3 is very unusual.
Leftovers view
Each row's miss plotted against X. A good fit looks like a random cloud around zero. Curves or funnel shapes mean a straight line isn't the whole story.

Good to know

  • It compares one X with one Y using a straight line. Curved relationships, several drivers at once, or trends over time need more advanced tools.
  • A strong pattern isn't proof that one thing causes the other.
  • Values like $1,250.00, (500) and 12% are read as numbers. Dates are read as YYYY-MM-DD or M/D/YYYY.
  • Row numbers match Excel: row 1 is the header, so the first data row is row 2.
  • The chart shows up to 5,000 dots so it stays fast. The math always uses every row.
  • Very large files (hundreds of MB) can be slow. Use Random sample on the Clean tab to speed things up.
